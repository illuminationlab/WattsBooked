Starting an electrical business?

Almost every electrician who starts a shop hits the same wall: the trade is the part you know cold, and everything around it is the part nobody taught you. Which license do you actually need? Do you register as an LLC? What do you charge? How do people even find you when you have zero reviews? The Roadmap

Six steps from licensed electrician
to a running electrical business.

01

Licensing, apprenticeship & insurance

There is no national license — it is set by your state and often your city. The path runs apprentice → journeyman → master, and most states want roughly 8,000 hours (about four years) plus classroom time before the journeyman exam. To hold a contractor license you usually need to be, or employ, a master electrician as the qualifying party. Then line up general liability, workers' comp if you have crew, and commercial auto — a small shop commonly runs a few thousand dollars a year in coverage.

02

Register the business

Pick an entity (many electricians choose an LLC for liability protection), file with your state, and get an EIN from the IRS so you can open a business bank account and hire. Then handle the trade-specific layer: your state electrical contractor registration or license, any local business license, and a surety bond where your state or municipality requires one — often in the $10,000–$25,000 bond range, with your actual cost only a small percentage of that.

03

Tools, van & startup costs

Budget in ranges, not guesses. A lean solo start often lands around $8,000–$25,000: a used service van ($12,000–$25,000 on its own if financed), a professional tool and tester set ($3,000–$8,000), safety gear, licensing and bond fees, and a little working capital. A more fully capitalized shop with crew can climb toward $60,000–$100,000. Start with what lands paying jobs and add specialized equipment as the work demands it.

04

Set your prices & quote your first jobs

Underpricing to win early work is the fastest way to burn out. Know your true hourly cost — labor, van, insurance, tools, and overhead — before you set a rate, then decide where you sit on flat-rate versus time-and-materials. Build a clean, repeatable estimate you can send fast, because the electrician who quotes first and follows up usually wins the job over the one with the lower number.

05

Get your first customers

Your first customers come from being findable and answering fast. Claim and fully complete your Google Business Profile, list your specific services (panel upgrades, EV chargers, rewires, emergency work), and ask for a review after every single job — the shops with the most reviews win the Local Pack. Add local marketing and a referral loop with real estate agents and property managers, who hit electrical needs constantly.

06

The systems that keep you from drowning

The jobs are not what sink new owners — the admin is. Missed calls while you are on the tools, quotes that never get chased, reviews you forget to ask for, invoices you send on Sunday night. From day one, put one system in place to answer calls, book jobs, follow up automatically, and request reviews — so the business runs whether you are up a ladder or off the clock.

To hold an electrical contractor license or registration in most states, you either are a master electrician or employ one as your qualifying party. Always confirm the exact hours, exams, and contractor-registration rules with your own state and city — they vary significantly.

It depends heavily on what you already own and how big you start. A properly capitalized small contractor with crew, multiple trucks, and several months of working capital can run $60,000 to $100,000 or more. Treat every figure as a range, not a quote — your market, vehicle, and reserve all move the number.
